Flush timeout setting, the maximum time it waits before writing to the destination. Partway through these measurements, the development team changed the WalShadow Flush timeout to 200 ms[5](#reference-5). The values in the table are from after the change.

I measured latency from "the moment a row is inserted and committed on the source" to "the moment the row is visible in the destination", using the same clock on the same laptop. I polled the destination every 0\.1 seconds. The table below shows three runs of 120 rows each at 1 row per second. I set the ClickPipes Sync interval to 60 seconds (the default), 10 seconds and 1 second, and measured WalShadow at the same time in every run. The docs recommend keeping the Sync interval above 10 seconds; I used 1 second only to see how it would behave[4](#reference-4).

| ClickPipes Sync interval | ClickPipes median / max | WalShadow (measured at the same time) median / max |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 s (default) | 31\.10 to 31\.14 s / 60\.62 to 60\.69 s | 0\.38 to 0\.40 s / 0\.49 to 0\.75 s |
| 10 s | 5\.56 to 5\.60 s / 10\.68 to 10\.74 s | 0\.38 to 0\.39 s / 0\.48 to 1\.42 s |
| 1 s | 1\.43 to 1\.44 s / 1\.72 to 1\.75 s | 0\.38 to 0\.40 s / 0\.47 to 0\.49 s |

ClickPipes latency is governed by the Sync interval you choose. The maximum was almost exactly the Sync interval. When there are more writes, ClickPipes starts writing at 100,000 rows without waiting the full 60 seconds, so latency drops even lower than at 1 row per second. If a delay of tens of seconds is fine for your use case, the default works as is, and if you need fresher data, you can shorten the interval, as long as the write rate stays within what the pipe can process (see below).
